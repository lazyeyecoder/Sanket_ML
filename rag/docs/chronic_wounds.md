## Diabetic foot/skin ulcers — general care
A diabetic ulcer should be cleaned gently, typically with soap and water unless a clinician has recommended a different cleanser, and dried by patting rather than rubbing. Avoid soaking the wound in a bath or whirlpool and avoid hydrogen peroxide, since these can slow healing and raise infection risk. Do not attempt to cut away or debride dead tissue yourself — debridement should only be done by a trained clinician. Because diabetes can blunt pain sensation and slow healing, a diabetic wound can worsen without feeling very painful, so it should be examined by a clinician promptly rather than monitored at home, especially if it has not visibly improved within about a month or shows any infection signs.

## Pressure ulcers (bed sores) — general care
The most important first step for a pressure ulcer is to relieve pressure on the area — help the person change position and avoid lying or sitting directly on the affected spot. Keep the surrounding skin clean, dry, and moisturized, patting rather than rubbing dry after cleaning. Keep bedding or seating smooth and free of creases. Watch for skin that is red or darker than surrounding skin and does not turn pale when pressed lightly (non-blanching), or that feels unusually hot, cold, or painful — these are early warning signs. Any pressure ulcer, or skin changes suggesting one, should be reported to a nurse or doctor promptly rather than managed alone, since pressure injuries can worsen quickly beneath the visible surface.
Source: NHS-affiliated pressure ulcer patient guidance — https://www.leedsth.nhs.uk/patients/resources/bed-sores-pressure-sores-and-pressure-ulcers/ ; https://www.cntw.nhs.uk/resources/pressure-ulcers-patient-information-leaflet

## Venous (leg) ulcers — general care
A venous ulcer is a slow-healing wound usually on the lower leg linked to poor vein circulation. Keep the wound clean and covered as directed by a clinician, and, if the person has been told to by a healthcare provider, elevating the leg above heart level when resting can help reduce swelling that slows healing. Do not attempt to independently apply compression bandaging unless you have been trained to, since incorrect compression can be harmful, particularly if arterial circulation problems have not been ruled out. Venous ulcers should be assessed by a clinician for proper wound care and treatment of the underlying circulation problem, especially if the wound is enlarging, increasingly painful, or shows infection signs.

## General principle for chronic/slow-healing wounds (diabetic, pressure, venous) [reference]
These wound types are not typically fixed by first aid alone — the priority for a bystander is: keep the area clean and protected, avoid unproven remedies or self-debridement, note any infection signs, and get the person to a clinician rather than waiting for it to resolve on its own.
