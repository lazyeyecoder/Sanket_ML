## Cooling a burn
Cool the burn under cool or cold running water for at least 20 minutes (many sources say 10-20 minutes minimum; do this as soon as possible after the injury). This reduces pain, swelling, and the risk of scarring. If clean running water is not available, use clean, lukewarm water or a cool, wet compress instead. Do not use ice or ice water — it is not recommended and can damage the skin further. Avoid immersing the entire body in cold water, since this risks hypothermia; cool only the burned area.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## What not to put on a burn
Do not apply butter, cream, oils, or toothpaste to a burn. Oils and fats retain heat, which works against cooling the burn. Do not use adhesive bandages directly on a burn, since they can stick to damaged skin and cause further injury when removed. Do not try to remove clothing or anything else that is stuck to the burn — leave it in place and let medical staff handle it.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## Covering a burn after cooling
After cooling the burn for the full duration, loosely cover it with cling film (placed lengthways, not wrapped tightly) or a clean plastic bag, or a clean, dry, non-stick/non-adhesive dressing. Covering loosely helps prevent infection and reduces pain by keeping air off the area. Remove any jewellery or tight clothing near the burn before swelling starts, unless it is stuck to the skin.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## When a burn needs emergency medical care
Seek emergency medical help for: any burn on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or a major joint; any burn larger than the person's palm; any burn that looks deep, white, leathery, or charred (suggesting a third-degree/full-thickness burn); burns from chemicals or electricity; and any burn on a baby or young child, regardless of size. Get immediate medical advice whenever you are uncertain about how serious a burn is.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## Burn severity by degree [reference]
A first-degree burn affects only the outer layer of skin — it is typically red, painful, and dry without blisters, and is generally the least severe visually. A second-degree (partial-thickness) burn goes deeper, often causing blisters, more intense pain, and swelling. A third-degree (full-thickness) burn destroys the full depth of skin and can look white, leathery, charred, or waxy; the area may be numb because nerve endings are damaged, which does not mean the burn is minor — third-degree burns are medical emergencies and always need professional treatment.
Source: General burn-classification guidance consistent with British Red Cross and international first aid guidelines — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns
