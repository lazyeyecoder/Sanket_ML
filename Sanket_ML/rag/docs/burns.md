## Cooling a burn
Summary: Cool the burn under running water for at least 20 minutes, as soon as possible.
- Cool the burn under cool or cold running water for at least 20 minutes, starting as soon as possible.
- If running water isn't available, use clean lukewarm water or a cool, wet cloth.
- Cool only the burned area - don't put the whole body in cold water.
- Do not use ice or ice water.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## What not to put on a burn
Summary: Keep oils, sticky bandages and stuck clothing away from the burn.
- Do not put butter, cream, oil or toothpaste on a burn.
- Do not stick adhesive bandages directly on a burn.
- Do not try to pull off clothing that is stuck to the burn - leave it for medical staff.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## Covering a burn after cooling
Summary: After cooling, cover the burn loosely with something clean and non-stick.
- After cooling, loosely cover the burn with cling film (laid lengthways, not wrapped tight), a clean plastic bag, or a clean, dry, non-stick dressing.
- Take off rings, jewellery or tight clothing near the burn before it swells, unless it is stuck to the skin.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## When a burn needs emergency medical care [urgent]
Summary: Deep, large, or high-risk-area burns need emergency help.
- Get emergency help for a burn on the face, hands, feet, genitals or a major joint, a burn larger than the person's palm, or one that looks deep, white, leathery or charred.
- Get emergency help for chemical or electrical burns, and for any burn on a baby or young child.
- If you are not sure how serious a burn is, get medical advice.
Source: British Red Cross, "First aid for burns" — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns

## Burn severity by degree [reference]
Summary: Burn degrees describe how deep the damage goes; third-degree burns are always emergencies.
A first-degree burn affects only the outer layer of skin - it is typically red, painful and dry without blisters. A second-degree (partial-thickness) burn goes deeper, often causing blisters, more intense pain and swelling. A third-degree (full-thickness) burn destroys the full depth of skin and can look white, leathery, charred or waxy; the area may be numb because nerves are damaged, which does not mean the burn is minor - third-degree burns always need professional treatment.
Source: General burn-classification guidance consistent with British Red Cross and international first aid guidelines — https://www.redcross.org.uk/first-aid/learn-first-aid/burns
