## Cleaning a minor cut, scrape, or abrasion
First clean your own hands. Rinse the wound under clean, running water to lower the risk of infection. Wash the skin around the wound with mild soap, but avoid getting soap directly inside the wound, and avoid hydrogen peroxide or iodine on the wound itself, since both can irritate the tissue and slow healing. If available, an antibiotic ointment or plain petroleum jelly can be applied afterward to keep the surface moist, then cover with a clean dressing.
Source: Consistent with Mayo Clinic and Red Cross minor-wound-care guidance — https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/wounds

## Signs a minor wound is becoming infected
Watch for: pain that gets worse a day or more after the injury (often the earliest sign); increasing redness, warmth, or swelling around the wound; pus or other drainage, especially if yellowish and foul-smelling; red streaks spreading from the wound up the limb; and fever. Any of these signs means medical care should be sought promptly, generally within a few hours rather than waiting.
Source: Consistent with Mayo Clinic and Red Cross minor-wound-care guidance — https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/wounds

## Abrasions specifically
An abrasion (a scrape where the top layer of skin is scraped away) should be rinsed thoroughly to remove any dirt or debris, since embedded debris increases infection risk. Gently pat dry rather than rub, then cover loosely. Abrasions are usually not an emergency on their own, but should still be watched for the infection signs above, and seen by a clinician if they are large, deep, dirty, or over a joint.
Source: General first-aid guidance consistent with Red Cross wound care — https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/wounds
